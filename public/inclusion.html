Access belongs in every journey.

12 / SOUTH AFRICAN SIGN LANGUAGE

Language access can also be visual.

SASL is a language in its own right. Plan human-produced SASL explanations for essential onboarding, rights, and consent information with Deaf community input. Provide captions and written alternatives for video, user-controlled playback, and an explicit option to load the media.

A translated text selector does not provide SASL access. Video interpretation, support arrangements, funding, and maintenance need their own delivery plan.

TRANSLATION SYSTEM

Translate the task, not just the menu.

Language support should be consistent, reviewed, and honest about its coverage.

Language choice

Present language names in their own written form, without flag icons. Let people choose before sign-in, change later, and retain the preference where appropriate. Never infer language from a person’s identity.

Complete critical journeys

Account creation, recovery, booking review, queue instructions, permission choices, errors, and help must all work in a supported language. A translated homepage alone is insufficient.

Human review

Maintain a glossary for clinic terms and patient-friendly wording. Work with qualified language reviewers and relevant care staff; test meaning with speakers rather than publishing unchecked machine translation.

Layout resilience

Allow longer translations and wrapping in buttons, navigation, alerts, and cards. Test names with diacritics, local date and number formatting, and meaningful field labels.

Transparent fallback

Mark untranslated content and offer a known language or a supported human help route. Do not silently switch critical permission or visit instructions to English.

Coverage register

Track language, journey, reviewer, version, and approval date. Prioritise releases with participating communities while keeping all 11 spoken languages and SASL in the full roadmap.

The language codes shown above are technical reference tags for the spoken-language interface. Use “Sepedi” as the displayed choice and validate local terminology with speakers. SASL video access should be named explicitly rather than offered as a text translation.

ACCESSIBILITY BASELINE

Make essential tasks accessible by default.

Target WCAG 2.2 Level AA for the production service, then validate with assistive technology and people who encounter barriers. A target is not a certification.

Readable contrast

Use at least 4.5:1 for ordinary text and 3:1 for qualifying large text. Keep sage for surfaces and accents where contrast is sufficient; use forest for readable text. Colour must never carry status alone. Contrast reference.

Keyboard & focus

Make every essential task possible by keyboard. Use a visible focus indicator, logical focus order, a skip link, and predictable focus after dialogs. Prevent sticky headers from hiding the active control.

Screen-reader meaning

Use landmarks, headings, labels, descriptive links, and text alternatives. Announce meaningful status changes politely; avoid reading every queue refresh. Distinguish decorative icons from controls.

Zoom & small screens

Keep ordinary content usable at a 320 CSS-pixel width without two-dimensional scrolling, subject to WCAG exceptions. Test zoom, text spacing, and long translated text. Reflow reference.

Touch & motor access

Design important touch controls around 44 × 44 CSS pixels where feasible. WCAG 2.2 AA’s minimum target criterion is 24 × 24 with spacing and other exceptions; it is not the same as the design target. Avoid drag-only tasks. Target size reference.

Reduced motion

Respect reduced-motion preferences, remove non-essential animation, and avoid flashing or attention-grabbing loops. Day/night artwork and pixel avatars remain optional decoration rather than instructions.

Hearing & media

Provide captions and transcripts for instructional audio/video, with SASL provision planned separately. A queue call must have readable instructions; sound alone is insufficient.

Cognitive clarity

Use short sentences, familiar words, one clear next action, and consistent help. Keep steps visible, explain terms, and ask only for information needed at that point. Errors should say how to recover.

Accessible authentication

Support password managers, autofill, and paste. Avoid making memory tests or puzzles the only route into an account. Offer an accessible recovery process. Authentication reference.

The baseline draws on W3C WCAG 2.2. Conformance needs a scoped audit of the finished service, not just individual colour or keyboard checks.

EVERYDAY LIMITATIONS

Design around the conditions of everyday life.

A person may face several barriers at once. Validate these scenarios with communities rather than using income, location, age, or disability as a shortcut for assumptions.

Stats SA’s General Household Survey 2025 reports fixed internet access at home for 33.5% of metropolitan households, compared with 2.7% of rural households. This measures fixed home access, not all internet use. It supports planning for different connectivity conditions; it does not describe any individual patient’s capability. GHS 2025, household internet access.

Limited data budgets

Prioritise lightweight text and essential task content. Lazy-load art; offer a low-data preference before media loads. Avoid autoplay, large background downloads, and mandatory map or video use.

Intermittent coverage

Keep the last confirmed state visible with a timestamp. Make retries safe and explain whether a request was received. Never turn a connection error into an empty appointments list.

Older phones & small screens

Test on lower-memory devices and supported older browsers. Use responsive single-column tasks, system-font fallbacks, progressive enhancement, and clear minimum support information.

Power & battery constraints

Avoid wasteful polling and heavy animation. Provide useful text instructions that can be read quickly. Let the clinic journey continue through confirmed staff-assisted processes.

Shared or borrowed devices

Make sign-out easy, mask sensitive information where appropriate, and prevent health data persisting after logout. Do not save medical records or secret tokens in general browser storage.

Limited digital familiarity

Show the next step in plain language, with optional contextual help. Use familiar labelled icons and forgiving forms; teach through the task instead of a long compulsory tutorial.

Contact or identity barriers

Do not assume every patient has a private email address, stable phone number, or identical identity documents. Define accepted enrolment and assisted recovery policies with the service owner.

Time, travel & vulnerability

Show confirmed opening hours, location, visit preparation, and realistic status. Avoid requiring a paid data connection to receive essential on-site instructions. Do not promise exact wait times.

Support from another person

Define authorised caregiver or representative access, consent, and revocation. Assistance must not depend on casually sharing a password or exposing records to whoever holds the phone.

CONNECTION STATES

When the connection drops, tell the truth.

The platform must distinguish confirmed information from stale or unsent work.

Confirmed

“Your appointment is confirmed.”

Show only after the service accepts it. Include the reference and visit details.

Not yet confirmed

“We could not confirm this request.”

Retain safe input where appropriate. Offer a retry that cannot accidentally create a second booking.

Stale queue data

“Last updated at 10:42. Reconnecting…”

Keep the last confirmed status visible and label its age. Advise the person to follow clinic instructions if the connection remains unavailable.

Unavailable detail

“We could not load this record.”

Offer retry and an appropriate help route. Do not substitute “no records” or show another patient’s information.

Assisted alternative

“Reception can help you check in.”

Publish only once that process is agreed with the clinic. Staff confirm the same underlying visit state.

Offline boundaries: offline reading of public help can be considered separately from protected records. Offline booking, check-in, and record access need explicit security, synchronisation, conflict, and service policies. They are not current capabilities.

ALTERNATIVE CHANNELS

Digital convenience must preserve a human route.

Plan and verify alternatives with participating clinics before describing them as available.

Staff-assisted care

Define reception-assisted registration, check-in, and account recovery. Preserve patient dignity and privacy; do not make app ownership a condition for clinic service.

SMS / USSD feasibility

Assess opt-in, device reach, message cost, delivery uncertainty, and operational support. Keep health details out of notifications. SMS or USSD support is a proposal, not an implemented feature.

Accessible support

Agree supported phone, in-person, written, and SASL assistance routes, hours, response ownership, and accessible escalation. Publish actual available channels only.

INCLUSION VALIDATION

Test with people, not assumptions.

Recruit across language, disability, device, connectivity, age, and literacy needs. Provide appropriate compensation and accessible participation.

  1. Observe essential tasks

    Test sign-in and recovery, booking review, check-in, queue instructions, record reading, permissions, and contact. Look for independent completion and understandable recovery.

  2. Test assistive technology and devices

    Use keyboard-only navigation, screen readers such as VoiceOver and TalkBack, zoom, high-contrast settings, reduced motion, and small screens. Include lower-end physical phones where possible.

  3. Test the difficult moments

    Introduce slow networks, dropped connections, expired sessions, lost codes, unavailable translations, and denied permissions. Confirm that the next step remains understandable.

  4. Publish an honest access statement

    List supported languages and devices, assessed scope, known limitations, feedback routes, and how barriers are handled. Update it as the service changes.
